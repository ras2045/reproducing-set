The Compression Ledger

Quantizing the real weights of a 1.48-billion-parameter language model, down to a 31-level integer codebook. What genuinely worked, what it loses to, and what a proper benchmark refuted outright.

What was actually built

Every 2D weight tensor in allenai/OLMo-2-0425-1B — 179 real tensors, 1,484,783,616 parameters — was quantized to a 31-level signed integer codebook with a per-32-value block scale, using a vectorized nearest-codeword search. The whole model went from 5,939.7 MB (fp32) to 1,176.0 MB, a real 5.051:1 ratio, in 410 seconds. Mean per-tensor cosine fidelity against the original weights: 0.997058.

That's not a synthetic benchmark. Every 2D tensor in the model was swapped for its quantized reconstruction and run through a real forward pass:

fp32: "The capital of France is Paris. The French language is spoken in France. The French people are known as the French. The"
quantized: "The capital of France is Paris. France is a country in Western Europe. It is a very old country. The French people"

Not identical, both fully coherent and factually correct. That's the expected, honest degree of degradation at ~5:1 lossy compression — not broken, not lossless.

The kernel

A real AVX2 gather+FMA kernel dequantizes and dot-products directly against the packed codebook representation, verified correct against numpy (relative RMSE 3.1×10−7). An earlier pshufb-based approach was rewritten with a genuinely vectorized byte-plane reassembly and got 2.5× faster than its first, buggy version — but still lost to the gather kernel. Both numbers are real, and the second one is a loss, reported as one.

What this loses to, honestly

llama.cpp's GGUF quantization

Not competitive

The codebook here is structurally the same idea as GGUF's own Q4_K/Q5_K formats — a per-block scale plus a quantization table, computed without calibration data. GGUF gets to a better effective bit-width (roughly 4–5 bits/value vs. this format's ~6) with a decade of kernel engineering and an enormous existing ecosystem behind it. This isn't a faster or smaller alternative to what's already free and dominant — it's an independent implementation of the same idea, at a worse operating point. Reported as such, not sold as more.

What a proper benchmark refuted

A real cache-hierarchy table looked like a design opportunity: attention-projection tensors fit entirely inside L3 cache once quantized, so tiling for L2/L3 residency looked like it should measurably help. It didn't. A real benchmark cycling the AVX2 kernel through 1 to 16 real quantized layers — 3.67 MB up to 58.72 MB, roughly 10× past the 6 MB L3 on this machine — held completely flat at ~5,585 cycles per row-dot at every single working-set size. Gather-instruction latency dominates regardless of whether the data fits in cache. The tiling claim isn't unproven anymore; it's refuted.

    flat 5582–5589 cycles/row-dot across 3.67MB–58.72MB, ~10× past L3 — no memory-bound regime found
  • pshufb faster than gather
    fixed reassembly bug, 2.5× faster than its buggy self, still ~1.7× slower than the gather kernel
