The Reproducing Set

A number whose own digits, fed back through repeated summation, eventually reproduce it. A multi-week search across 3–17 digits, two results that survived rigorous testing — and a public record of everything that didn't.

The definition

Take a number n with d digits. Seed a sequence with those d digits as the first terms. Every later term is the sum of the previous d terms — a generalized Fibonacci recurrence, width d instead of 2. n is self-reproducing if it reappears later in its own sequence, before the sequence grows past 50× its own value.

seed (d=3): 1, 9, 7  → 17, 33, 57, 107, 197
197 reappeared — 197 is self-reproducing.

What the search found

Starting from exhaustive brute force and ending with a custom meet-in-the-middle solver, the search covered every digit-length from 3 to 17, turning up 57 confirmed self-reproducing numbers. The rate of discovery collapses fast — each additional digit doesn't just add one more decimal place, it makes hits roughly ten times rarer. Ten digits produced none at all.

Two real discoveries

Most of what got tested came back statistically ordinary. Two things didn't — one is a proof, the other is a measured, repeatable signature with no proof yet.

The additive family rule

Proven

Because the recurrence is linear, two self-reproducing numbers of the same length can be added digit-by-digit — no carrying — to produce a third. This isn't a pattern noticed in the data; it follows directly from the math.

1104 + 2580 = 3684, digit by digit — each digit drawn as its own shape (0 wild, 1 circle, 2 line, 3–9 an n-sided polygon)

The smoothness signature

Measured, unexplained

Self-reproducing numbers are consistently far more composite than random numbers of the same size — their largest prime factor is typically 10–100× smaller, relative to n, than a random-integer control. The gap widens as digit-length grows. There's a plausible mechanism (summing recurrence terms tends to accumulate small factors) but no proof.

The 18-tooth fact

A separate, fully proven result from the same line of research: among gaps between consecutive primes, only 10 of 18 possible values of gap mod 18 ever occur — 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16. Confirmed at a real scale of 300,000,000 (16,252,325 primes, zero counterexamples).

The mechanism is exact, not statistical: a gap between two odd primes is always even, so its residue must be one of the 9 even values mod 18. The sole exception is the very first gap, 2→3, which equals 1. Nine even residues plus one odd exception is ten — the gear above has no room for an eleventh.

What didn't hold up

Every item below was tested against the real data and came back statistically indistinguishable from chance. Ruling these out is real information — it's what let the two findings above stand out as genuine.

  • Modular residues
    mod 3, 7, 9, 11, 13 — all χ² well under significance
  • Digit 9 frequency & position
    9.6% occurrence vs. 10% expected
  • Benford's law (leading digit)
    χ²=11.3 of 8 dof, threshold 15.5

    no pair correlation found
  • Density vs. prime gaps
    decay matches known prime + hit density curves independently
  • Digit-shape "interlocking"
    Tested 2026-09-08
    Same shape rule as above (digit → polygon interior angle), tested as a real geometric closure condition: does a number's running angle-sum ever land on an exact multiple of 360°? Real rate 1.7% vs. 20,000-trial structure-space control mean 0.7% — real assignment ranks 210th of 20,001 (p≈0.03 on one metric, p≈0.08 on a second, correlated one, driven by only 6 of 57 numbers). Not distinguishable from noise at this sample size — no real interlocking effect.

Tooling built along the way

An exhaustive C search validated the method through 12 digits (a 27-hour run on 8 cores), but exhaustive search is fundamentally exponential. A meet-in-the-middle solver, exploiting the fact that "self-reproducing at step k" is a single linear equation in the seed digits, cut that to roughly the square root of the search space and pushed the reachable range to 17 digits.

A further attempt to reach much larger digit-lengths using lattice reduction (LLL) — the technique behind classic knapsack-cipher attacks — failed for a genuine mathematical reason: the recurrence's own structure fills the lattice with small unintended relations. That's the same failure mode that broke the Merkle–Hellman knapsack cipher in 1982. An encryption scheme built directly on this recurrence would very likely rediscover that weakness rather than escape it — worth naming, not worth building.
